# RES-109: Survey: criteria, thresholds and false alarms for a check that stops an agent

## Question
Survey, area 3 of 3: how do proven sources write the criteria of a blocking check, set its threshold from data, and keep false alarms low, for the nine survey questions?

## Answer
This is a map of proven practice. It is not a recommendation. It covers 21 source rows (44 pages) in six groups: model makers, research on the ask, threshold method, rollout practice, alarm fatigue, and lint and policy practice.

How the pages were read: each page came through a tool that returns raw page text (a search tool with page extracts, a page fetch, or a direct download). No quote comes from a summarized page. Large PDFs were read through the abstract page or the HTML page only.

### Where the sources agree

1. Criteria are narrow and binary, and both sides are defined. S2, S4, S6 and S9 ask a yes/no question for one behavior. S2 adds what does not count. S8 uses a fixed list of four conditions.
2. The test set has cases on both sides. S2, S4 and S6 require cases where the check must fire and cases where it must not. S2, S5 and S6 require human labels as the reference.
3. The threshold comes from the cost of the two errors on data the check did not see. S4, S15, S16 and S18 use this method. S14 adds two time measures.
4. Observe before you block. S8, S16 and S17 run the check without effect first. S4 and S12 use a staged roll-out with monitoring.
5. A check that blocks needs a much lower false alarm rate than a check that advises. S8 allows none against below 10%. S14 pages against tickets. S16 blocks against reviews.
6. Two stages are common: a sensitive first stage, then a precise second stage. S1, S9 and S14 use this shape.
7. A stop is useful when it says what is wrong and what to do next. S1, S8 and S21 state this. S8 and S21 add a channel to report a bad stop.
8. The moment of a question matters. S18 and S20 show that a question at a task boundary is accepted more often.
9. The check needs a feedback measure in use. S8 counts "Not useful" clicks. S9 counts later edits. S21 records the bypass reason.

### Where the sources disagree

| Topic | One position | Other position |
|---|---|---|
| Yes/no against a score | S2, S4 and S6: yes/no is more reliable | S4 cookbook blocks at score 3 of 5. The Anthropic eval skill file says a binary grade "throws away the shape of the tradeoff". S5 supports both |
| Acceptable false alarm rate | S8: none for a block, below 10% for advice | S1: 0.4% of benign actions. S6: its targets permit 10% to 20% false fails (my reading). S9 ran at 65% to 75% precision. One proposal in S11: at most 40% overrides, no basis given |
| What the rate counts | S1 counts blocked benign actions out of all benign actions | S8, S9 and S11 count stops that the person did not act on, out of all stops. These numbers cannot be compared |
| Precision or recall first | S8 and S9: precision first, because noise destroys trust | S1 stage 1: recall first. S4: depends on the harm of a miss |
| Number of cases | S6: about 100 with an expert | Anthropic docs: more cases with automated grading. S1 had only 52 real positive cases. S7: criteria move while you label |
| Does better text help | S8: a new message text stopped the bug reports | S12: new text gave +1.2 points; layout gave the gain. S11: forced override reasons did not raise acceptance |
| A way to proceed | S21 and S12: a bypass exists and costs effort | S8: no bypass at the blocking level, so no false alarm is allowed there |
| More or fewer questions | S19 Ambig-SWE: agents ask too seldom | S19 Ask Less, Ask Better and S20: more questions give small gain and more burden. The value of each question matters more than the count |

Evidence against popular claims:
- "Fewer alarms is safer" is not always true. In S10, wider limits cut alarms but more true events occurred. In S1, the second stage cut false alarms and misses rose from 6.6% to 17%.
- "False positive" hides two cases. S13 found that most were true alarms with a benign cause. S8 counts a correct alarm that nobody understood as a false positive.
- "Write clear criteria first" has a limit. S7 found that people change the criteria while they grade.
- "The agent cannot judge when to ask" is partly wrong. S3 and S19 (Ask or Assume) show some calibration. S19 (Ambig-SWE) shows that agents fail to ask by default.

### Worth a deep run

1. S1, Claude Code auto mode. It is the nearest case: a model check that blocks an agent's next action and reads the user's messages for consent. It has measured rates on real traffic. Its main miss, "is this consent for this action", is the same class as our three moments.
2. Judge validation, S2 with S6 and S7. These give the full method for a labelled set and the test of the judge. A deep run must find how to label "the agent had to stop here" and if two people agree on it.
3. S8, Google static analysis. It gives the clearest bar for a check that blocks against a check that advises, the "effective false positive" measure, and a feedback loop that disables bad checks.
4. S19 with S18, the research on the ask. It gives a cost model for a question, a detector that is separate from the worker agent, and evidence on question budgets.
5. Rollout mechanics, S16 and S17. Replay on past data and a record-only mode are directly usable on session transcripts. The open point is the criterion for the move to blocking.

### Not reached or not verified

- UNVERIFIED: on the most complex goals the agent asked in 16.4% of turns and humans interrupted in 7.1%. Two secondary pages state this. The primary page shows a figure and states only "more than twice as often".
- UNVERIFIED: a 0.97 rate of failure to detect an underspecified task for one model in Ambig-SWE. Only a slide page of an author shows it.
- UNVERIFIED: about 100 cases give a margin near 10 points and about 400 give near 5 points. A secondary page attributes this to a vendor article. No primary statistics source was read for sample size.
- Not reached: HashiCorp Sentinel enforcement levels, Content-Security-Policy report-only mode, ESLint and Semgrep rule levels, the Joint Commission alarm goal, studies of interruption cost in office work, and studies of the calibration of a model's own confidence score.
- No source states a measured criterion for the move from log-only to blocking. Secondary guides say "after violations reach zero or an accepted level".
- No source measures a check for the exact three moments in our brief.

## Findings
- S1: Users accept 93% of manual permission prompts in Claude Code, and the post names approval fatigue as the result. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-auto-mode, checked 2026-11-21.
- S1: The classifier runs in two stages: a single-token yes/no filter told to err toward blocking, then chain-of-thought reasoning only on flagged actions. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-auto-mode, checked 2026-11-21.
- S1: The classifier prompt is a fixed template with three slots for policy (trusted environment, block categories, exceptions), and it checks block rules, then exceptions, then user intent. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-auto-mode, checked 2026-11-21.
- S1: Read-only tools and file edits inside the project skip the classifier, so only actions with real downside reach it. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-auto-mode, checked 2026-11-21.
- S1: The test data were 10,000 real tool calls, 52 real overeager actions (denied by an employee or flagged later) and 1,000 synthetic exfiltration cases. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-auto-mode, checked 2026-11-21.
- S1: Stage 1 alone gave an 8.5% false positive rate and a 6.6% false negative rate on real overeager actions; the full pipeline gave 0.4% and 17%. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-auto-mode, checked 2026-11-21.
- S1: In most misses the classifier saw the danger but judged wrongly that a consent signal covered the action, and the authors found no prompt fix that did not cost more than it gained. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-auto-mode, checked 2026-11-21.
- S1: The classifier sees only user messages and tool calls; adding assistant text and tool results reduced performance, and a tool-calls-only variant blocked actions the user had plainly asked for. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-auto-mode, checked 2026-11-21.
- S1: A denial returns to the agent as a tool result with the reason and an instruction to find a safer path and not route around the block. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-auto-mode, checked 2026-11-21.
- S1: After 3 consecutive denials or 20 in total the system stops the model and escalates to the human, and a false positive then costs one retry and not the session. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-auto-mode, checked 2026-11-21.
- S2: A good task is one where two domain experts independently reach the same pass/fail verdict, and vague rubrics produce inconsistent judgments. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents, checked 2026-11-21.
- S2: Test sets must cover cases where a behavior should occur and where it should not, because one-sided evals create one-sided optimization; the web search example needed many rounds to balance undertriggering and overtriggering. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents, checked 2026-11-21.
- S2: The guide recommends deterministic graders where possible, model graders where necessary, close calibration of a model judge with human experts, an "Unknown" answer for the judge, and one isolated judge for each dimension. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents, checked 2026-11-21.
- S2: The guide says "Make criteria atomic and binary", prefers a set of yes/no questions to one overall score, and says to state what does not meet the criterion. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-strong-safety-evaluations, checked 2026-11-21.
- S2: Before a grader is trusted, humans label a sample without the grader's verdicts, and the agreement is reported corrected for chance and compared with the agreement between the human labelers. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-strong-safety-evaluations, checked 2026-11-21.
- S2: Grader verdicts can change when the underlying model changes, so the guide asks for alerts on grader failure and a second grading of a sample with a different model or rubric wording. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-strong-safety-evaluations, checked 2026-11-21.
- S2: If human experts disagree on the correct answer, the guide says no right answer exists for the grader and the measure must be reconsidered. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-strong-safety-evaluations, checked 2026-11-21.
- S2: The Anthropic docs list Likert scales among the valid measurement methods, and say more questions with automated grading are better than fewer questions graded by hand. Source: https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests, checked 2026-11-21.
- S2: An Anthropic eval skill file says to write a rubric as concrete checkable claims and not vague scales, but for a trade-off to prefer a continuous measure because "a binary grade throws away the shape of the tradeoff". Source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/anthropics/skills/main/skills/claude-api/shared/evals/build-eval.md, checked 2026-11-21.
- S3: On the most complex tasks Claude Code asks for clarification more than twice as often as on minimal-complexity tasks, and more often than humans interrupt it (500k sessions). Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/measuring-agent-autonomy, checked 2026-11-21.
- S3: The agent stops itself to offer a choice between approaches (35%), to get diagnostic information (21%), to clarify a vague request (13%), to request credentials (12%) and to get approval before an action (11%). Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/measuring-agent-autonomy, checked 2026-11-21.
- S3: Humans interrupt most often to give missing technical context or corrections (32%) and because the agent was slow, hanging or excessive (17%). Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/measuring-agent-autonomy, checked 2026-11-21.
- S3: The authors state that the agent "may not be stopping at the right moments" and "may ask unnecessary questions". Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/measuring-agent-autonomy, checked 2026-11-21.
- S4: OpenAI recommends pairwise comparison or pass/fail for a model judge "for more reliability", reasoning before the score, and a check of agreement against human labels. Source: https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evaluation-best-practices, checked 2026-11-21.
- S4: OpenAI names two triggers for human intervention: a failure limit is exceeded (retries or actions), or the action is high-risk (sensitive, irreversible or high stakes). Source: https://openai.com/business/guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-ai-agents/, checked 2026-11-21.
- S4: OpenAI rates each tool low, medium or high by write access, reversibility, permissions and financial impact, and uses the rating to pause for a check or escalate to a human. Source: https://openai.com/business/guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-ai-agents/, checked 2026-11-21.
- S4: An OpenAI cookbook blocks output that scores 3 or higher on a 1 to 5 scale and says to set the threshold with an evaluation set and a confusion matrix. Source: https://github.com/openai/openai-cookbook/blob/main/examples/How_to_use_guardrails.ipynb, checked 2026-11-21.
- S4: The same cookbook states the trade-off: more false positives annoy users, more false negatives can cause lasting harm, and a strict guardrail causes over-refusals. Source: https://github.com/openai/openai-cookbook/blob/main/examples/How_to_use_guardrails.ipynb, checked 2026-11-21.
- S4: The same cookbook recommends a gradual roll-out with active monitoring of conversations. Source: https://github.com/openai/openai-cookbook/blob/main/examples/How_to_use_guardrails.ipynb, checked 2026-11-21.
- S4: A second OpenAI cookbook says to balance positive and negative examples for each guardrail and to run the evals on each policy change; its demo targets 90% precision and 90% recall with sets of 21 and 16 samples. Source: https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/partners/agentic_governance_guide/agentic_governance_cookbook, checked 2026-11-21.
- S5: Google Cloud says to prepare a dataset with human ratings as ground truth and to compare the judge with it by balanced accuracy, balanced F1 and a confusion matrix; it gives no minimum size and no pass bar. Source: https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/models/evaluate-judge-model, checked 2026-11-21.
- S5: Google Cloud recommends 4 samples for each judge call to reduce randomness and flips response order to reduce position bias. Source: https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/configure-judge-model, checked 2026-11-21.
- S6: A practitioner guide (opinion) asks for about 100 traces with Pass/Fail labels for each failure mode, about 50 of each, labelled by a domain expert and not by outsourced annotators. Source: https://github.com/hamelsmu/evals-skills/blob/main/skills/validate-evaluator/SKILL.md, checked 2026-11-21.
- S6: The same guide splits the labels into train (10% to 20%), dev (40% to 45%) and test (40% to 45%), uses the test set once, and targets a true positive rate and a true negative rate above 90% with 80% as the minimum. Source: https://github.com/hamelsmu/evals-skills/blob/main/skills/validate-evaluator/SKILL.md, checked 2026-11-21.
- S6: The same guide says that below 60 labelled examples the confidence intervals become wide. Source: https://github.com/hamelsmu/evals-skills/blob/main/skills/validate-evaluator/SKILL.md, checked 2026-11-21.
- S6: The authors' FAQ (read on a secondary host) says binary labels force a decision, while on a 1 to 5 scale annotators differ on adjacent points and default to middle values. Source: https://www.gregorojstersek.com/evals-faq.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- S7: In a study with 9 practitioners, people changed their criteria while they graded outputs ("criteria drift"), and some criteria depended on the outputs seen. Source: https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3654777.3676450, checked 2026-11-21.
- S7: The study measures an evaluator by coverage of bad outputs and by false failure rate against the user's grades, and one participant wanted a different false failure limit for each criterion. Source: https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3654777.3676450, checked 2026-11-21.
- S8: Google calls an issue an "effective false positive" when developers took no positive action after they saw it, also when the issue was real but not understood. Source: https://cacm.acm.org/research/lessons-from-building-static-analysis-tools-at-google/, checked 2026-11-21.
- S8: A check that breaks the build must be easily understood, actionable with a suggested fix where possible, produce no effective false positives, and report only correctness issues. Source: https://cacm.acm.org/research/lessons-from-building-static-analysis-tools-at-google/, checked 2026-11-21.
- S8: A check shown in code review may produce up to 10% effective false positives. Source: https://cacm.acm.org/research/lessons-from-building-static-analysis-tools-at-google/, checked 2026-11-21.
- S8: Before a check blocks, the team runs it on the whole codebase, sends fixes for all existing cases, and only then enables it as a compiler error; plain warnings were ignored by developers. Source: https://cacm.acm.org/research/lessons-from-building-static-analysis-tools-at-google/, checked 2026-11-21.
- S8: Surveyed developers judged 74% of issues flagged at compile time as real problems, against 21% of issues found in checked-in code. Source: https://cacm.acm.org/research/lessons-from-building-static-analysis-tools-at-google/, checked 2026-11-21.
- S8: The overall effective false positive rate of the code review platform is just below 5%. Source: https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch20.html, checked 2026-11-21.
- S8: A "Not useful" button files a bug to the analyzer's owner, and the team disables analyzers that do not improve a high "Not useful" rate. Source: https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch20.html, checked 2026-11-21.
- S8: In one case weekly "Not useful" reports stopped after the team changed only the text of the message. Source: https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch20.html, checked 2026-11-21.
- S9: A code review bot at ByteDance uses two stages, a rule checker and a filter that answers yes or no for each comment, and puts precision before recall because developers ignore the comments when there are many, and wrong comments damage trust. Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15134, checked 2026-11-21.
- S9: On 1,397 labelled cases the filter raised precision from 57.03% to 65.59% and recall fell from 45.50% to 39.77% (table values; the text of the paper gives 54.50% to 67.12%). Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15134, checked 2026-11-21.
- S9: The team tracks an "Outdated Rate", the share of flagged lines changed later, which reached 26.7% against 35% to 46% for human reviewers, and removes rules with low precision or a low Outdated Rate. Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15134, checked 2026-11-21.
- S10: A systematic review of 32 studies found that 74% to 99% of hospital monitor alarms were not actionable, and two small studies linked high alarm exposure to longer nurse response time. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4778561/, checked 2026-11-21.
- S10: In one randomized trial a lower oxygen alarm limit gave 61% fewer alarms but more true low-oxygen events of more than one minute (6% against 2%). Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4778561/, checked 2026-11-21.
- S10: The review calls wider alarm limits, alarm delays and better electrode practice promising but unproven, and says many of the studies had a high risk of bias. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4778561/, checked 2026-11-21.
- S11: A review of 17 papers found that clinicians override drug safety alerts in 49% to 96% of cases, and names low specificity, unclear information and unnecessary workflow disruption as causes. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/, checked 2026-11-21.
- S11: The same review reports a proposed maximum override rate of 40% and says its authors gave no explanation for the figure. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/, checked 2026-11-21.
- S11: A review of 39 articles found that interruptive pop-up alerts were accepted less often than other designs (38.67% against 61.57%), and that only role tailoring appeared to raise acceptance. Source: https://academic.oup.com/jamia/article/26/10/1141/5519579, checked 2026-11-21.

## Follow-ups
- Which rate do we use for a false alarm: stops on good actions out of all good actions (as S1), or stops the user did not want out of all stops (as S8)?
- Do past session transcripts hold enough real cases of each of the three moments to label about 50 positive cases for each?
- Do two people give the same label to "the agent had to stop here"? S2 says the check has no reference if they do not.
- Is the check a hard stop, or a denial that the agent can recover from as in S1? The acceptable false alarm rate depends on this.
- Can the check run on past transcripts in a record-only mode, and what result decides the move to blocking? No source gives a measured criterion.

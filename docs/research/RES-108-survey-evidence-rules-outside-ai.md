# RES-108: Survey: evidence rules and research method outside AI

<!-- Add outcome: answered, partial or blocked to frontmatter when an answer or a block is known. Only answered work can become resolved. -->

## Question
Survey, area 4 of 5: which evidence rules and research methods do established fields outside AI use, for the nine survey questions?

## Answer
**The survey covers 16 methods from 11 fields. All are written, public and in use.** The table gives one row per method and one column per survey question. "Not stated" means that the pages read are silent; it does not mean that the field has no rule.

Three words in this record have the meaning of the fields, not the meaning of Brain's glossary. A "protocol" is the written plan of one research run. A "claim" is a statement that needs support. A "step" of a method is not a lifecycle stage.

### Where the methods agree
- **The plan is written before the search.** Six methods require it. The scoping review and the UK guide also require a record of each later change.
- **The size is a decision with stated criteria.** The criteria are the use of the result, the risk, the time and the amount of evidence. The UK guide, the software guideline and the BBC state them most clearly.
- **Quality of a source counts more than the number of sources.** The audit standard, the Tradecraft Primer and the journalism critic say this in almost the same words.
- **Two things get separate grades.** The Admiralty code separates the source from the information. GRADE separates certainty from the strength of a recommendation. ICD 203 separates likelihood from confidence.
- **The report states what is not known.** Gaps, uncertainty and contested evidence are a required part of the result in ICD 203, the Berkeley Protocol, the BBC rules and the UK guide.
- **The researcher looks for evidence against the favored answer.** Examples are alternative hypotheses, the "Drawbacks" section, exonerating information and contradicting audit evidence.
- **A second person checks, and the check shrinks with the size.** It goes from two independent people, to one person plus a check of a part, to approval by a supervisor.
- **Past work comes first where the method names it.** The software guideline, the UK guide, the rapid review and the legal guide start from existing work or confirm that none exists.
- **In health and policy, the decision maker gets a short table, not the full record.** The table has a few outcomes or statements, each with its certainty.

### Where the methods disagree
- **One authoritative primary source against several secondary sources.** Journalism, audit and investigation rank first-hand and original material highest. Wikipedia requires secondary sources and limits primary ones. Law and the rapid review start from secondary sources for orientation and then go to primary sources.
- **The number of sources.** Journalism gives numbers, with an approved exception for one authoritative source. Wikipedia asks for more sources only for an exceptional claim. Audit and intelligence refuse a number. A full review wants all sources.
- **The rule for "enough".** The rules are: all evidence, saturation, a fixed effort, repetition of the same sources, risk, or the deadline. The Cochrane Handbook says that no firm rule exists for the search.
- **Grading of sources.** A scoping review does not grade its sources and so must not support a decision. The larger sizes grade each source.
- **A fixed plan against iteration.** Cochrane fixes the method in advance. The scoping review, the Berkeley Protocol and the software guideline expect iteration and record each change.
- **One researcher.** Cochrane refuses a review by one person. The software guideline gives rules for one researcher. The rapid review permits one person plus verification.
- **Structured techniques.** ICD 203 requires analysis of alternatives. One test found that Analysis of Competing Hypotheses, a technique for it, did not improve accuracy.
- **Checklist against a look outside.** CRAAP judges a source by questions about the source. Lateral reading and SIFT leave the source and check what others say.
- **The "two sources" rule.** The news agencies make it a requirement for anonymous sources only. Its critic says that a count has no value without a standard for each source and without independence between the sources.

### Worth a deep run
1. **The size ladders: the UK guide and the Cochrane rapid review guidance.** They name sizes, the criteria to select one, the permitted short cuts per size, and the role of the user. This is the closest match to research that serves one decision. The 2024 update of the rapid review guidance was not read.
2. **The software engineering guidelines (Kitchenham and Charters; Garousi and others).** They cover web sources of mixed quality, a check of existing reviews with a checklist, rules for one researcher, and three stop rules. This is the closest match to research on tools and libraries.
3. **The report to the decision maker: ICD 203, GRADE and the UK evidence statements.** Each one separates the support for a statement from the choice that follows. A deep run must include ICD 206 on source statements and the GRADE Evidence to Decision framework, which were not read.
4. **Rules for existing work: current, update or redo.** The sources are the BMJ checklist of 2016, Cochrane chapter IV, JBI section 10.4 and the checklists in the software guideline. They fit the question of how to judge an old research record.
5. **Source independence, and primary against secondary sources.** The fields disagree here, and a rule such as "never one source" needs this ground. The sources are the journalism codes, Wikipedia policy, the audit standard and the Admiralty code.

## Findings
- Cochrane review: the methods "should be established and documented in advance" in a protocol, and "the review question should determine the methods used in the review, and not vice versa" (section 1.5). Source: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-01, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane review: a team does the review. "Cochrane will not publish a review that is proposed to be undertaken by a single person", and selection, data extraction and certainty ratings are done "by at least two people independently" (section 1.3). Source: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-01, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane review: chapter 1 names no step that searches for an existing review of the same question. It says that a published protocol and a PROSPERO registration reduce "duplication of effort, research waste" (section 1.5). Source: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-01, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane review: some sources are mandatory. CENTRAL, MEDLINE and Embase must be searched (standard C24), trials registers must be searched (C27), and the reference lists of included studies must be checked (C30). Grey literature (C28) is "highly desirable", not mandatory. Source: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-04, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane review: the search is run by one specialist, and a second specialist reviews the search strategy before it runs. Two independent searches are "not considered necessary (or even desirable)" (section 4.2.2); peer review of the strategy with the PRESS checklist is "strongly recommended" (section 4.4.8). Source: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-04, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane review: at least two people decide independently if a study is eligible (C39, mandatory). The process for disagreements is defined in advance: discussion first, then "arbitration by another person"; an open case is listed as awaiting assessment (section 4.6.4). Source: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-04, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane review: there is no firm rule for when to stop a search. Section 4.4.11 gives suggestions only, for example to stop when new search terms yield "no new relevant records", and says these approaches have had little formal evaluation. Source: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-04, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane review: a search older than 12 months, preferably 6, must be run again before publication (C37, mandatory), and the search must be documented "exactly as run and in full" (C36, section 4.5). Source: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-04, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane review: automatic removal of records by a tool "has not been recommended for use in Cochrane reviews", and tools based on large language models lacked large, valid evaluations when the chapter was written (section 4.6.6.2, chapter last updated March 2025). Source: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-04, checked 2026-11-21.
- GRADE in Cochrane: a body of evidence gets one of four certainty levels: high, moderate, low or very low. Randomized trials start at high and non-randomized studies start at low. Five domains lower the level (risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias) and three raise it (section 14.2). Source: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-14, checked 2026-11-21.
- GRADE in Cochrane: the decision maker gets a "Summary of findings" table with at most seven outcomes, in order of importance, with good and bad outcomes, relative and absolute effects, the amount of evidence, the certainty level and the reasons for it (sections 14.1.3, 14.1.6). Two people should rate the certainty independently and then agree (C74). Source: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-14, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane update: the decision to update a review asks three things in order. Is the question still relevant to decision makers? Was the review done well with suitable methods? Would new studies or new methods change the findings or the credibility? There is "no consensus on when to update a review" (section IV.2). Source: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-iv, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane update: when the old review used poor methods, "it may be more appropriate to conduct a new review from scratch". An update with no new studies is still published, because the new search date reassures readers (sections IV.2, IV.4). Source: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-iv, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane update: a living systematic review updates continually, for example with a monthly search. It fits important questions where new evidence appears often, and "considerable resources are required" (section IV.1). Source: https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-iv, checked 2026-11-21.
- Update checklist (Garner and others, BMJ 2016): step 1 asks if the review still addresses a current question and used valid methods; step 2 looks for new methods, studies or information; step 3 asks if these change the findings or the credibility. Outcomes named are update, postpone, no update needed, archive, or continue surveillance. A review with vague criteria or wrong methods is not updated; a new protocol starts. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4955793/, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane rapid review (interim guidance, 2021): a rapid review is "a form of knowledge synthesis that accelerates the process of conducting a traditional systematic review" by streamlining or omitting methods. It is for "urgent and high priority questions" and is "driven by the need for timely evidence for decision-making purposes". Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7557165/, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane rapid review: the short cuts are named one by one. Search three main databases and limit grey literature; two reviewers screen at least 20% of abstracts, then one reviewer screens and a second reviewer checks all exclusions; one reviewer extracts data and rates bias and certainty, and a second reviewer verifies. Screening by one reviewer alone is not recommended. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7557165/, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane rapid review: the people who will use the result set and refine the question, the criteria and the outcomes, and are consulted "throughout the process to ensure the research question is fit for purpose". A protocol is still required. Existing systematic reviews are used first where available. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7557165/, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cochrane rapid review: the authors state that many of the short cuts lack evidence, and that the guidance rests in part on a survey with 63 answers. A 2024 update with 24 recommendations replaces the 26 of 2021 (secondary: search result summary, not read in full). Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7557165/ and https://www.bmj.com/content/384/bmj-2023-076335, checked 2026-11-21.
- JBI scoping review: a systematic review is "likely the most appropriate choice" when the question is about effectiveness or feasibility, or the review must inform practice or policy. A scoping review maps the extent and nature of the evidence for a broad or exploratory question (section 10.1.6). Source: https://jbi-global-wiki.refined.site/space/MANUAL/2043052033, checked 2026-11-21.
- JBI scoping review: an a priori protocol is "a requirement". A change is permitted because the work is iterative, but "deviations from the protocol with supporting justification should be made transparent" (section 10.2.2). Source: https://jbi-global-wiki.refined.site/space/MANUAL/355862667, checked 2026-11-21.
- US intelligence standard ICD 203 (2015, amended 2022 and 2023): the standards are applied to each product "in a manner appropriate to its purpose, the type and scope of its underlying source information, its production timeline, and its customers" (section D.1). Source: https://www.odni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-203.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- ICD 203: a product must describe the "quality and credibility of underlying sources, data, and methodologies". The factors named include accuracy, completeness, possible deception, age of the information, and the source's access, motivation, bias and expertise (D.6.e.1). Source: https://www.odni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-203.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- ICD 203: likelihood and confidence are two separate things. Likelihood uses a fixed table of seven terms with percent ranges, from "almost no chance" (01-05%) to "almost certain" (95-99%). A confidence level and a likelihood term must not be in the same sentence (D.6.e.2). Source: https://www.odni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-203.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- ICD 203: a product must separate source information from the analyst's assumptions and judgments, state the assumptions that are the linchpin of an argument, and "identify and assess plausible alternative hypotheses" (D.6.e.3 and D.6.e.4). Source: https://www.odni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-203.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- ICD 203: a product must state how its judgments agree with or change earlier published analysis, and significant differences between two analytic units must be "brought to the attention of customers" (D.6.e.7). Source: https://www.odni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-203.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- ICD 203: analysis "should be informed by all relevant information available", and the unit must "identify and address critical information gaps" (D.6.d). Checks are a regular review program of finished products in each agency and an independent Analytic Ombuds for concerns (sections D.2, E.2). ICD 203 states no number of sources that a judgment needs. Source: https://www.odni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-203.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- Tradecraft Primer (US Government, March 2009): it groups structured techniques by purpose. Diagnostic techniques make assumptions and gaps visible, contrarian techniques challenge current thinking, and imaginative techniques develop new outcomes. It gives a timeline that maps each technique to the start, the middle or the end of a project (pages 5, 38). Source: https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Tradecraft-Primer-apr09.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- Tradecraft Primer: "Having multiple sources on an issue is not a substitute for having good information that has been thoroughly examined." The Quality of Information Check reviews all sources for accuracy, finds the most critical ones and checks them for "sufficient and strong corroboration" (pages 10-11). Source: https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Tradecraft-Primer-apr09.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- Tradecraft Primer: Analysis of Competing Hypotheses lists all hypotheses, puts each item of evidence in a matrix against them, and tells the analyst to "focus on disproving hypotheses rather than proving one". Evidence that fits all hypotheses has no value for the choice. The report gives all conclusions, "including the weaker hypotheses" (pages 14-15). Source: https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Tradecraft-Primer-apr09.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- Tradecraft Primer: it states its own limit. The techniques alone are "no guarantee of analytic precision or accuracy of judgments" (page 5). It quotes the US review of the Iraq assessments: "over-reliance on a single, ambiguous source" (page 11). Source: https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Tradecraft-Primer-apr09.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- AP news agency: "The AP routinely seeks and requires more than one source when sourcing is anonymous." One source is enough only "in rare cases", when an authoritative figure gives information "so detailed that there is no question of its accuracy". A news manager must approve and must know who the source is. Source: https://www.ap.org/about/news-values-and-principles/telling-the-story/, checked 2026-11-21.
- AP news agency: a story from another outlet that rests on anonymous sources is not a confirmation. AP must first try to get it on the record "or, at a minimum, confirm it through our own reporting". Source: https://www.ap.org/about/news-values-and-principles/telling-the-story/, checked 2026-11-21.
- Reuters Handbook of Journalism (April 2008 edition, copy on a third-party host): "Two or more sources are better than one." Stories from anonymous sources "should normally have two or three sources". A story from one anonymous source is "the exception", needs a supervisor's approval, and the source must be a participant "with first-hand knowledge". Source: https://www.mediareform.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/Reuters_Handbook_of_Journalism.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- Reuters Handbook: it ranks sources. Named sources come first; "the weakest sources are those whose names we cannot publish"; "reliability declines the further away the source is from the event". It says "never cite sources in the plural when you have only one source", and a report picked up from another outlet must still be checked. Source: https://www.mediareform.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/Reuters_Handbook_of_Journalism.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- BBC Editorial Guidelines: accuracy is "due", which means "adequate and appropriate to the output", so the effort fits the subject and the kind of content (section 3.1). Source: https://www.bbc.co.uk/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy, checked 2026-11-21.
- BBC Editorial Guidelines: producers gather from first-hand sources, corroborate claims, and "should be reluctant to rely on a single source". A plan to rely on a single unnamed source for a serious allegation must go to the Director Editorial Policy and Standards before use (sections 3.4.1 to 3.4.4, 3.3.1). Source: https://www.bbc.co.uk/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines, checked 2026-11-21.
- BBC Editorial Guidelines: what is not verified is said to be not verified and is attributed (3.4.6). Research records are kept so that another team member can check them again (3.4.15). Source: https://www.bbc.co.uk/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines, checked 2026-11-21.
- JBI scoping review: "ideally" at least two people screen each title and abstract independently, and "screening by a single reviewer is not recommended, unless you are considering a rapid scoping review". When in doubt, the reviewer includes the source for the full-text step. Each exclusion at full text needs a reason (section 10.2.7). Source: https://jbi-global-wiki.refined.site/space/MANUAL/355862769, checked 2026-11-21.
- JBI scoping review: it does not grade its sources. "We do not advocate for the use of critical appraisal or risk-of-bias assessment" in a scoping review, and where it is done the result is descriptive and must not support a recommendation for practice or policy (section 10.2.8). Source: https://jbi-global-wiki.refined.site/space/MANUAL/355862791, checked 2026-11-21.
- JBI scoping review: the search is comprehensive and includes grey literature, which "is particularly useful in emerging fields". The protocol sets the procedure that answers "How will you know whether you have searched enough?", to prevent "ad hoc decision-making during your search" (section 10.2.6). Source: https://jbi-global-wiki.refined.site/space/MANUAL/355862749, checked 2026-11-21.
- JBI scoping review: an update is considered when new sources appear, when practice or policy changes, or "every two to three years" by the speed of the field. The authors check first if an update is already in progress (section 10.4). Source: https://jbi-global-wiki.refined.site/space/MANUAL/355863308, checked 2026-11-21.
- JBI scoping review: known problems are the wrong choice of review type, poor quality of some reviews, and conclusions that are "overstretched". JBI treats 12,000 search hits or about 100 included sources as large, and then narrows the question, splits the review or adds people (section 10.5). Source: https://jbi-global-wiki.refined.site/space/MANUAL/2052030485, checked 2026-11-21.
- Admiralty code (secondary sources only; the US Army manual FM 2-22.3 that they cite was not read): a report gets two ratings. A letter from A to F rates the reliability of the source, and a number from 1 to 6 rates the credibility of the information. "Each descriptor is considered in isolation" so that the source's reliability does not influence the assessed accuracy of the report. Rating 1 means "confirmed by other independent sources". Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiralty_code, checked 2026-11-21.
- Admiralty code, criticism (secondary, one author, 2026): the code measures credibility, not accuracy; corroboration is often not possible when there is "only a single authoritative source"; it "makes no allowance for information age"; and the grades are subjective, with "no criteria with which to distinguish a C from a D rating". Source: https://smallwarsjournal.com/2026/10/02/beyond-the-admiralty-code-rethinking-information-evaluation-civil-affairs/, checked 2026-11-21.
- Software engineering review (Kitchenham and Charters, version 2.3, 2007): before a review starts, researchers "should identify and review any existing systematic reviews of the phenomenon of interest against appropriate evaluation criteria". It gives two checklists for that judgment, one with four questions on criteria, search coverage, quality assessment and description of the studies (section 5.1). Source: https://legacyfileshare.elsevier.com/promis_misc/525444systematicreviewsguide.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- Software engineering review: it has three sizes. A systematic mapping study (a scoping study) fits when "very little evidence is likely to exist or that the topic is very broad". A tertiary review, a review of reviews, fits where several reviews exist already and is "potentially less resource intensive". A full review is the third (section 2.5). Source: https://legacyfileshare.elsevier.com/promis_misc/525444systematicreviewsguide.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- Software engineering review: "a pre-defined protocol is necessary to reduce the possibility of researcher bias". The protocol holds the questions, the search strategy with the resources to search, the selection criteria, the number of assessors, the way to resolve disagreements, the quality checklists and the data extraction plan. Piloting the protocol "is essential" (section 5.4). Source: https://legacyfileshare.elsevier.com/promis_misc/525444systematicreviewsguide.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- Software engineering review: the steps look sequential but "many of the stages involve iteration", and search strategies "are usually iterative". Preliminary searches find existing reviews and estimate the volume of studies (sections 4, 6.1.1). Source: https://legacyfileshare.elsevier.com/promis_misc/525444systematicreviewsguide.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- Software engineering review: it has a rule for one researcher. A single researcher discusses included and excluded papers with an advisor or an expert panel, or evaluates a random sample again later (test-retest). With two or more researchers, "each disagreement must be discussed and resolved" and the agreement statistic is reported (section 6.2.3). Source: https://legacyfileshare.elsevier.com/promis_misc/525444systematicreviewsguide.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- Software engineering review: it reports that the fixed hierarchy of evidence from medicine was called "too simplistic", because the best study design depends on the type of question (section 6.3.1). Its stated main cost: such reviews "require considerably more effort than traditional literature reviews" (section 2.3). Source: https://legacyfileshare.elsevier.com/promis_misc/525444systematicreviewsguide.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- Multivocal review (Garousi, Felderer and Mäntylä, 2018 preprint): a multivocal review adds grey literature, such as blog posts and white papers, to formal papers. Seven yes-or-no questions decide if grey literature is included, for example a lack of volume or quality in the formal literature, or important context from practice. One "yes" suggests inclusion (guideline 3, table 4). Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.02553, checked 2026-11-21.
- Multivocal review: it gives three rules to stop a web search. "Theoretical saturation, i.e., when no new concepts emerge from the search results"; "effort bounded, i.e., only include the top N search engine hits"; and "evidence exhaustion, i.e., extract all the evidence" (guideline 8). Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.02553, checked 2026-11-21.
- Multivocal review: grey sources are graded on the authority of the producer, method, objectivity, date, novelty, impact and outlet control. Outlet control has three tiers: books, magazines and government reports are high; annual reports, news articles, Q/A sites and wiki articles are moderate; blogs, emails and tweets are low (guideline 11, table 7). It also says to identify existing reviews first (guideline 2). Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.02553, checked 2026-11-21.
- Rust RFC template: a proposal must have the sections "Drawbacks" ("Why should we not do this?"), "Rationale and alternatives" ("What other designs have been considered and what is the rationale for not choosing them?"), "Prior art" ("both the good and the bad") and "Unresolved questions". It adds that precedent in other languages "does not on its own motivate an RFC". Source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rust-lang/rfcs/master/0000-template.md, checked 2026-11-21.
- Design docs at Google (one engineer's account, 2020): a design doc is written when the solution "is ambiguous"; a doc with no trade-off is an "implementation manual" and is not needed. "Alternatives considered" is "one of the most important" sections. Review is light (comments from a team list) or heavy (a meeting with senior engineers), and the author warns that review can become "a dangerous trap of overhead". Source: https://www.industrialempathy.com/posts/design-docs-at-google/, checked 2026-11-21.
- Thoughtworks Technology Radar: a group of about 18 senior technologists places each technology in one of four rings: Adopt, Trial, Assess, Caution. The ring is a grade of evidence from own use: Trial needs "experience of using that blip for production software". An entry that does not move "fades from the Radar". It states its limit: "no attempt is made to be comprehensive or to survey the market at large". Source: https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/faq, checked 2026-11-21.
- Genealogical Proof Standard (Board for Certification of Genealogists): a conclusion is credible only with five parts. "Reasonably exhaustive research", "complete and accurate source citations", "thorough analysis and correlation", "resolution of conflicting evidence", and a "soundly written conclusion based on the strongest evidence". The manual is Genealogy Standards, second edition revised, 2021. Source: https://bcgcertification.org/ethics-standards/, checked 2026-11-21.
- CRAAP test (Meriam Library, California State University, Chico, 2010): a list of questions on five criteria: Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose. It grades one source at a time and gives no score or pass mark, because "different criteria will be more or less important depending on your situation or need". Source: https://library.csuchico.edu/sites/default/files/craap-test.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- SIFT (Caulfield, 2019): four moves. Stop; investigate the source; find better coverage; trace claims, quotes and media to the original context. It fits effort to purpose: "Quick and shallow investigations will form most of what we do on the web". For a claim, it tells the reader to "ignore the source that reached you" and look for the best coverage of the claim. Source: https://hapgood.us/2019/06/19/sift-the-four-moves/, checked 2026-11-21.
- Lateral reading (Wineburg and McGrew, Stanford; search result summary of the abstract, paper not read in full): in a study of 45 people, historians and students judged a website by its own features and were often misled. Fact checkers left the site, checked what other sites said about it, and "arrived at more warranted conclusions in a fraction of the time". Source: https://cor.inquirygroup.org/research/lateral-reading-and-the-nature-of-expertise, checked 2026-11-21.
- Wikipedia policy: it prefers secondary sources. Articles are based on "reliable, published secondary sources"; "any interpretation of primary source material requires a reliable secondary source"; and editors must not combine sources "to state or imply a conclusion not explicitly stated" by any of them. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No_original_research, checked 2026-11-21.
- Wikipedia policy: the number of sources depends on the claim. One inline citation to a reliable source meets the burden for normal content, but "any exceptional claim requires multiple high-quality sources". The burden lies "with the editor who adds or restores material". Wikipedia itself is not a source for its articles. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability, checked 2026-11-21.
- Audit standard PCAOB AS 1105: the amount of evidence follows risk and quality. "As the risk increases, the amount of evidence that the auditor should obtain also increases." "As the quality of the evidence increases, the need for additional corroborating evidence decreases." More of the same type "cannot compensate for the poor quality of that evidence" (paragraph .05). Source: https://pcaobus.org/oversight/standards/auditing-standards/details/AS1105, checked 2026-11-21.
- Audit standard PCAOB AS 1105: it ranks evidence by origin. An independent, knowledgeable source is more reliable than an internal one. Evidence obtained directly by the auditor is more reliable than indirect evidence. Original documents are more reliable than copies (paragraph .08). Inquiry alone "does not provide sufficient audit evidence" (paragraph .17). Source: https://pcaobus.org/oversight/standards/auditing-standards/details/AS1105, checked 2026-11-21.
- Audit standard PCAOB AS 1105: evidence includes information that contradicts the claim (paragraph .02). If evidence from one source is inconsistent with another, or its reliability is in doubt, the auditor "should perform the audit procedures necessary to resolve the matter" (paragraph .29). Source: https://pcaobus.org/oversight/standards/auditing-standards/details/AS1105, checked 2026-11-21.
- Berkeley Protocol (UN human rights office and UC Berkeley, 2022): open source investigation is a cycle of six steps: online inquiry, preliminary assessment, collection, preservation, verification, investigative analysis. The work is "rarely linear" and often repeats. Analysis includes "identifying gaps for further investigation" (chapter VI). Source: https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/2024-01/OHCHR_BerkeleyProtocol.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- Berkeley Protocol: a written online investigation plan comes first and "should be reviewed on a regular basis and amended as necessary" (paragraph 122). Assumptions are recorded as assumptions, and the known gaps are named at the start, to "prevent biased or skewed outcomes". Search terms cover "multiple theories of the case" and both incriminating and exonerating information (chapter VI.A). Source: https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/2024-01/OHCHR_BerkeleyProtocol.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- Berkeley Protocol: verification looks at three things together: the source, the digital item and the content (paragraph 176). The record must let the investigator "explain the steps taken, or not taken" and why (paragraph 121). A report states its gaps or uncertainties, and "peer review is recommended" (chapter VII, note 168). Source: https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/2024-01/OHCHR_BerkeleyProtocol.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- PRISMA 2020: it is a reporting standard with 27 items, not a method. It "is not intended to guide systematic review conduct" and "should not be used to assess the conduct or methodological quality" of a review. A report must name every source searched and the date, give the full search strategies, and say "how many reviewers screened each record" and "whether they worked independently". Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8005924/, checked 2026-11-21.
- GRADE Handbook (updated October 2013; a GRADE Book replaces it since 2024): the strength of a recommendation is a separate judgment from the certainty of the evidence. Values, resource use, equity, acceptability and feasibility can change it, and a strong recommendation on low certainty is possible. Detailed evidence profiles serve reviewers; the shorter Summary of findings table serves "a broader audience". The text read gives no voting rule; it says reviewers will have "legitimate disagreement" and must make the reasoning explicit. Source: https://gdt.gradepro.org/app/handbook/handbook.html, checked 2026-11-21.
- UK government evidence review guide (Collins and others, 2015; published on GOV.UK 2016): it gives four sizes with times. A literature review takes 1-2 weeks, a quick scoping review 3-5 months, a rapid evidence assessment 5-8 months, and a systematic review 10-18 months. The user selects the size "taking a risk-based approach"; a systematic review "may be disproportionate for a relatively 'low risk' topic". Source: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7f3a76ed915d74e33f5206/Production_of_quick_scoping_reviews_and_rapid_evidence_assessments.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- UK government evidence review guide: a quick scoping review does not appraise quality, so it serves only "general understanding of the evidence base". If the output will "directly inform a decision", a rapid evidence assessment or a systematic review with critical appraisal is required (step 1). Source: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7f3a76ed915d74e33f5206/Production_of_quick_scoping_reviews_and_rapid_evidence_assessments.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- UK government evidence review guide: the user of the result is part of the process. The user must "confirm the work has not been previously undertaken", approves the protocol, agrees each change to it, and discusses the findings with a steering group to "identify if further work is" needed. An Overall Assurer checks the method. One person screens all results and a second person screens a part independently. Source: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7f3a76ed915d74e33f5206/Production_of_quick_scoping_reviews_and_rapid_evidence_assessments.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- UK government evidence review guide: findings go to the decision maker as numbered evidence statements, each with a confidence class from the quality and the quantity of the studies: High, Medium, Low or Contested. Contested means "evidence that differs in its conclusions", and the class of each study is shown. A non-technical summary is required (sections 11, 12). Source: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7f3a76ed915d74e33f5206/Production_of_quick_scoping_reviews_and_rapid_evidence_assessments.pdf, checked 2026-11-21.
- Criticism of systematic reviews (Ioannidis, Milbank Quarterly 2016): of 73 meta-analyses from 2010, 67% had at least one more on the same topic by the end of 2012; one topic had 21. Only about 10% to 20% of reviews are registered in advance. The author concludes that "few systematic reviews and meta-analyses are both non-misleading and useful". Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5020151/, checked 2026-11-21.
- Cost of systematic reviews (Borah and others, BMJ Open 2017): "The mean estimated time to complete the project and publish the review was 67.3 weeks", with a mean of 5 authors per review. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5337708/, checked 2026-11-21.
- Criticism of Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (Mandel, Karvetski and Dhami, Judgment and Decision Making 2018): in a test with intelligence analysts, "ACH failed to improve accuracy", and the control group was a little more accurate. The authors say there "has been little effort to test" the effectiveness of structured analytic techniques. Source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/boosting-intelligence-analysts-judgment-accuracy-what-works-what-fails/1530E9DAE8F42B2E8FC2B5FE374DB50F, checked 2026-11-21.
- Criticism of the two-source rule (Mollenhoff, Nieman Reports 1981): "Any type of 'two-source' or 'three-source' rule is nonsense unless there is a sound standard" for the credibility of each source. "One good solid source, a direct witness with no axe to grind" is better than several sources who pass on hearsay, and people who heard the same story are not independent corroboration. Source: https://niemanreports.org/?p=3222, checked 2026-11-21.
- Legal research (Boston College Law Library guide): the process has five steps: read and think, consult guides, use secondary sources, find primary sources, assemble and update. Secondary sources come first to "orient ourselves in a new area of law", but "primary authority is the law itself". The last step checks with a citator that each primary source is "still good law". The work "is an iterative process". Source: https://lawguides.bc.edu/tips/TheResearchProcess, checked 2026-11-21.
- Legal research: the rule for enough is repetition. "A good rule of thumb is to stop when you seem to be getting the same few sources over and over again", and only after the question is answered "in the appropriate format". The answer is "context dependent". Source: https://lawguides.bc.edu/tips/WhenToStop, checked 2026-11-21.
